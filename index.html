Topic 1: Divisibility Rules

Fast checks for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10 & 11 without long division

Standard Divisibility Rules (Factually Exact)

  • by 2 The unit digit is even: 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8.
  • by 3 The sum of all digits is divisible by 3.
  • by 4 The number formed by the last two digits is divisible by 4 (or ends in 00).
  • by 5 The unit digit is either 0 or 5.
  • by 6 The number is divisible by both 2 AND 3 (it is even and its digit sum is a multiple of 3).
  • by 8 The number formed by the last three digits is divisible by 8 (or ends in 000).
  • by 9 The sum of all digits is divisible by 9.
  • by 10 The unit digit is strictly 0.
  • by 11 The difference between the sum of digits at odd places and sum of digits at even places (counted from right to left) is 0 or a multiple of 11.
⚡ Classmate's Memory Shortcut: Co-prime Combinations & Tail Truncation

• Composite numbers: If a number is divisible by two co-prime factors, it is divisible by their product! Check 6 via (2 and 3); check 12 via (3 and 4); check 15 via (3 and 5).
• Zero-effort tests: For 2, 5, and 10, completely ignore 99% of the number — only check the final digit! For 4 and 8, you only ever need the last 2 or 3 digits.

Why It Works: The Place-Value Remainder Secret

Ever wondered why summing digits works for 3 and 9? Write any 4-digit number \(abcd\) in expanded place value:

N = 1000a + 100b + 10c + d
N = (999 + 1)a + (99 + 1)b + (9 + 1)c + d
N = [999a + 99b + 9c] + (a + b + c + d)
N = 9(111a + 11b + c) + (a + b + c + d)

The first bracket \(9(111a + 11b + c)\) is guaranteed to be divisible by 9 (and 3). Therefore, whether \(N\) is divisible depends entirely on the leftover piece: the digit sum \((a + b + c + d)\)!

Worked Examples: Step-by-Step Tests

Example 1: Check 4,572 for divisibility by 3 and 8
• For 3: Sum digits = 4 + 5 + 7 + 2 = 18.
• 18 ÷ 3 = 6 (No remainder!).
✓ Divisible by 3 (and 9)!
• For 8: Last 3 digits are 572.
• 572 ÷ 8 = 71 with remainder 4.
✗ NOT Divisible by 8
Example 2: Check 2,915 for divisibility by 11
• Digits from right: 5 (pos 1), 1 (pos 2), 9 (pos 3), 2 (pos 4).
• Odd place sum = 5 + 9 = 14.
• Even place sum = 1 + 2 = 3.
• Difference = 14 − 3 = 11.
✓ Divisible by 11 (11 × 1 = 11)

Topic 2: Algebra: 3 Identities + 1 Property

Geometric area proofs, mental expansion tricks, and substitution drills

The 3 Standard Identities + 1 Bonus Property

  • Identity I (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
  • Identity II (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
  • Identity III (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
  • Property IV (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab
⚡ Peer Shortcut: "Square First, Square Last, Double the Middle"

• For \((a \pm b)^2\): Just square \(a\) \(\to a^2\), square \(b\) \(\to +b^2\), and multiply \(a \times b\) doubled \(\to \pm 2ab\). (Remember: the last term \(+b^2\) is ALWAYS positive!)
• Fast Mental Multiplication: Spot "sum × difference" around round numbers! E.g. \(47 \times 53 = (50 - 3)(50 + 3) = 50^2 - 3^2 = 2500 - 9 = 2,491\). No scratchpad needed!

Why It Works: The Geometric Area Intuition

Algebra isn't just symbols — it's geometry! A square with side \((a + b)\) has a total area of \((a + b)^2\). When you partition its sides into \(a\) and \(b\), the square naturally divides into 4 distinct physical regions: one large square of area \(a^2\), one small square of area \(b^2\), and two identical rectangles each of area \(ab\). Adding them together yields \(a^2 + 2ab + b^2\)!

Worked Examples: Real Examination Problems

• Rewrite: 102² = (100 + 2)²

• 100² + 2(100)(2) + 2² = 10,000 + 400 + 4

• Rewrite: 98² = (100 − 2)²
• Substitute: a = 100, b = 2
• 100² − 2(100)(2) + 2² = 10,000 − 400 + 4
= 9,604

Topic 3: Congruence of Triangles

SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS criteria & interactive superimposition demo

The 5 Exact Congruence Criteria

  • SSS Side-Side-Side: All three corresponding sides of two triangles are equal.
  • SAS Side-Angle-Side: Two sides and the included angle between them are equal.
  • ASA Angle-Side-Angle: Two angles and the included side between them are equal.
  • AAS Angle-Angle-Side: Two angles and any corresponding non-included side are equal.
  • RHS Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side: In right-angled triangles, the hypotenuse and one leg are equal.
⚠️ Student Trap Alert & The CPCT Superpower

• The SSA Trap: There is NO "SSA" (Side-Side-Angle) criterion in maths! Two sides and a non-included angle can swing to produce two entirely different triangles (the ambiguous case).
• CPCT Magic: The second you prove two triangles congruent, write: "By CPCT (Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles)" — this immediately unlocks equality for all remaining sides and angles for free!

Worked Example: Complete Examination Proof

Given: In △ABC and △PQR, AB = PQ, BC = QR, and AC = PR. Prove △ABC ≅ △PQR.
Proof:
In △ABC and △PQR:
• AB = PQ    (Given)
• BC = QR    (Given)
• AC = PR    (Given)
∴ △ABC ≅ △PQR (By SSS Congruence Criterion)
Hence by CPCT:
∠A = ∠P,  ∠B = ∠Q,  ∠C = ∠R

Topic 4: Squares, Square Roots, Cubes & Cube Roots

Prime factorization grouping, unit-digit rules, 4×4 dot grid & 3D isometric cube

Core Mathematical Rules & Unit Digit Patterns

  • Square Rule Forbidden Endings: A perfect square never ends in 2, 3, 7, or 8! They can only end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9.
  • Square Roots Prime Factor Pairs: Express the number as product of primes; group identical primes into pairs (groups of 2) and take one factor per pair.
  • Cube Roots Prime Factor Triplets: Group identical primes into triplets (groups of 3) and take one factor per triplet.
  • Cube Digits Unit Digit Fingerprint: 1 ends in 1, 4 in 4, 5 in 5, 6 in 6, 9 in 9, 0 in 0. For the others, they pair up to 10: 2 ↔ 8 and 3 ↔ 7!
⚡ Fast Estimation Shortcut: Unit-Digit + Range Bracket

To find \(\sqrt{576}\) mentally in 3 seconds:
1. Bracket the tens: \(20^2 = 400\) and \(30^2 = 900\), so the root is between 20 and 30 (tens digit = 2).
2. Unit digit clue: 576 ends in 6, meaning its root must end in either 4 (\(4^2=16\)) or 6 (\(6^2=36\)).
3. Since 576 is much closer to 400 than 900, the answer is immediately 24!

Why Factor Pairing/Grouping Works (Exponent Law)

By the fundamental laws of exponents: \((p^k)^2 = p^{2k}\). Squaring any integer doubles every exponent in its prime factorization! Therefore, taking a square root is simply halving the exponents (pairing primes). Similarly, \((p^k)^3 = p^{3k}\). Cubing triples every exponent, so taking a cube root is dividing the exponents by 3 (grouping in triplets).

Worked Examples: Prime Factorization Method

Find √225 via Prime Factorization
• Step 1: 225 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5
• Step 2: Group pairs = (3 × 3) × (5 × 5)
• Step 3: Take one factor per pair = 3 × 5
√225 = 15
Find ∛343 via Prime Factorization
• Step 1: 343 = 7 × 7 × 7
• Step 2: Group in triplets = (7 × 7 × 7)
• Step 3: Take one factor per triplet = 7
∛343 = 7

Steps: 1. Pair digits from right (\(\overline{50}\ \overline{41}\)). 2. Largest square \(\le 50\) is \(7^2=49\). 3. Remainder \(1\), bring down next pair \(41 \rightarrow 141\). 4. Double quotient (\(7 \times 2 = 14\)), attach digit \(1\) (\(141 \times 1 = 141\)).

Steps: Split into groups of 3 from right (\(17\) and \(576\)). The last group \(576\) ends in \(6\), so units digit is \(6\). The first group \(17\) sits between \(2^3=8\) and \(3^3=27\), so tens digit is \(2\).

Topic 5: Linear Equations & Graphs

Balancing scales, transposition rules, cross-multiplication & coordinate lines

Fundamental Balancing Axioms & Solution Methods

  • Balance Axiom An equation is a balanced scale. Whatever you do to the Left-Hand Side (LHS), you MUST do identically to the Right-Hand Side (RHS). \(A = B \iff A \pm c = B \pm c\).
  • Transposition Moving a term across the \(=\) sign flips its operational sign: \(+\) becomes \(-\), \(-\) becomes \(+\), \(\times\) becomes \(\div\), and \(\div\) becomes \(\times\).
  • Rational Form For equations reducible to linear form \(\frac{ax + b}{cx + d} = \frac{p}{q}\), cross-multiply: \(q(ax + b) = p(cx + d)\) (where \(cx + d \ne 0\)).
  • Graph Slope-Intercept Any linear relation in two variables graphs as a straight line: \(y = mx + c\), where \(m = \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\) is slope and \((0, c)\) is the \(y\)-intercept.
⚡ Classmate's Shortcut: The LCM Fraction-Nuker & Negative Sign Trap

• Fraction-Nuker: Never battle common denominators term-by-term! Find the LCM of all denominators in the equation and multiply every single term on BOTH sides by that LCM. Fractions vanish in 1 second!
• The Negative Bracket Trap: When expanding \(-(3x - 5)\), remember that the minus sign acts as \((-1)\). It distributes to BOTH terms: \(-3x + 5\) (never \(-3x - 5\)).

Why Cross-Multiplication Works (Rigorous Proof)

Given the rational fraction equation:

\[\frac{ax + b}{cx + d} = \frac{p}{q}\] Multiply both sides simultaneously by \((cx + d) \cdot q\):
\[\left[\frac{ax + b}{cx + d}\right] \times (cx + d) \cdot q = \left[\frac{p}{q}\right] \times (cx + d) \cdot q\] Cancel the matching denominators on each side:
\[(ax + b) \cdot q = p \cdot (cx + d)\]

This eliminates the denominators completely, yielding a standard single-line linear equation with no division needed!

Worked Examples: Step-by-Step Tests

Example 1: Rational Linear Equation
Solve: \[\frac{x}{2} - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{x}{3} + \frac{1}{4}\]
• Step 1 (Transpose terms): Collect variable terms on LHS and constants on RHS: \[\frac{x}{2} - \frac{x}{3} = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{5}\]
• Step 2 (Common Denominators via LCM):
Since \(\text{LCM}(2, 3) = 6\) and \(\text{LCM}(4, 5) = 20\): \[\frac{3x - 2x}{6} = \frac{5 + 4}{20} \implies \frac{x}{6} = \frac{9}{20}\]
• Step 3 (Isolate \(x\)): Multiply both sides by 6: \[x = \frac{9 \times 6}{20} = \frac{54}{20} = \frac{27}{10}\]
✓ Solution: \(x = \frac{27}{10} = 2.7\)
Example 2: Classic Age Word Problem
Problem: A mother is 26 years older than her son. In 3 years, her age will be 3 times her son's age. Find their present ages.
• Step 1 (Assign variables):
Let the Son's present age be \(x\) years.
Then the Mother's present age \(= (x + 26)\) years.
• Step 2 (Ages after 3 years):
Son's age in 3 years \(= x + 3\)
Mother's age in 3 years \(= (x + 26) + 3 = x + 29\)
• Step 3 (Form and solve equation): \[x + 29 = 3(x + 3)\] \[x + 29 = 3x + 9\] Transpose variables to RHS and numbers to LHS: \[29 - 9 = 3x - x \implies 20 = 2x \implies x = 10\]
✓ Son is 10 years old; Mother is 36 years old (\(10 + 26 = 36\))!

Topic 6: Exponents & Powers

The 7 laws of exponents, negative powers, scientific notation & Olympiad tricks

The 7 Core Laws of Exponents (Master Table)

  • Law 1: Product \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\)  (When bases are identical, add the exponents).
  • Law 2: Quotient \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\)  (When bases are identical, subtract the exponents, \(a \ne 0\)).
  • Law 3: Power of Power \((a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n}\)  (Multiply exponents when stacked).
  • Law 4: Product Base \((ab)^m = a^m \times b^m\)  and  \(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^m = \frac{a^m}{b^m}\) (\(b \ne 0\)).
  • Law 5: Zero Power \(a^0 = 1\)  for any non-zero real number \(a\).
  • Law 6: Negative Exponent \(a^{-m} = \frac{1}{a^m}\)  and  \(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-m} = \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^m\).
  • Law 7: Standard Form Any number can be expressed as \(k \times 10^n\) where \(1 \le k < 10\) and \(n\) is an integer.
⚡ Classmate's Shortcut: The Olympiad Power Factorization Trick

• Factoring Common Powers: When simplifying sums like \(2^{25} - 2^{24}\), never compute large powers! Pull out the smallest power as a common factor:
\[2^{25} - 2^{24} = 2^{24}(2^1 - 1) = 2^{24}(1) = 2^{24}!\] • Base Sign Rule: \((-1)^{\text{even}} = +1\) whereas \((-1)^{\text{odd}} = -1\). Watch out: \(-3^2 = -(3^2) = -9\), but \((-3)^2 = +9\)!

Why \(a^0 = 1\) and \(a^{-1} = \frac{1}{a}\): The Division Pattern

Observe powers of 2 stepping downwards by dividing by 2 at each step:

\(2^3 = 8\)
\(2^2 = 8 \div 2 = 4\)
\(2^1 = 4 \div 2 = 2\)
\(2^0 = 2 \div 2 = 1\)  (Proof that any non-zero base to power 0 must be 1!)
\(2^{-1} = 1 \div 2 = \frac{1}{2}\)
\(2^{-2} = \frac{1}{2} \div 2 = \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2^2}\)

Also by Law 2: \(\frac{a^m}{a^m} = a^{m-m} = a^0\). But any non-zero quantity divided by itself is 1. Hence, \(a^0 = 1\) identically!

Worked Examples: Simplification & Standard Form

Example 1: Simplify \(\frac{25 \times t^{-4}}{5^{-3} \times 10 \times t^{-8}}\)
• Step 1: Write all bases as powers of 5: \(25 = 5^2\), \(10 = 2 \times 5^1\).
• Step 2: Group powers of 5: \(\frac{5^2}{5^{-3} \times 5^1} = \frac{5^2}{5^{-2}} = 5^{2 - (-2)} = 5^4 = 625\).
• Step 3: Group powers of \(t\): \(\frac{t^{-4}}{t^{-8}} = t^{-4 - (-8)} = t^4\).
• Step 4: Include remaining 2 in denominator.
✓ Simplified: \(\frac{625 t^4}{2}\)
Example 2: Standard Form (Scientific Notation)
• Distance from Earth to Sun: \(149,600,000\text{ km}\) \(\implies 1.496 \times 10^8\text{ km}\). (Decimal shifted 8 places left).
• Size of plant cell: \(0.00001275\text{ m}\) \(\implies 1.275 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}\). (Decimal shifted 5 places right).
• Ratio comparison: Sun distance is \(\approx 10^{13}\) times larger than a plant cell!
✓ Standard form simplifies microscopic & galactic calculations.

Topic 7: Quadrilaterals & Polygons

Angle sum theorem, 360° exterior invariant, full family hierarchy & diagonal proofs

Polygon Theorems & Quadrilateral Classification

  • Interior Sum Sum of interior angles of an \(n\)-sided polygon \(= (n - 2) \times 180^\circ\). (For a quadrilateral, \((4-2)\times 180^\circ = 360^\circ\)).
  • Exterior Invariant The sum of exterior angles of ANY convex polygon is ALWAYS strictly \(360^\circ\), regardless of the number of sides!
  • Regular Polygon Each interior angle \(= \frac{(n-2)\times 180^\circ}{n}\). Each exterior angle \(= \frac{360^\circ}{n}\).
  • Parallelogram Opposite sides are parallel & equal; opposite angles are equal; adjacent angles are supplementary (\(180^\circ\)); diagonals bisect each other.
  • Rhombus & Kite In a Rhombus, all 4 sides are equal, and diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other (\(90^\circ\)). In a Kite, diagonals are perpendicular.
⚡ Classmate's Shortcut: The 360° Exterior Hack & Diagonal Counter

• Exterior Angle Hack: If an exam asks: "Find sides of a regular polygon with interior angle \(160^\circ\)", never solve \(\frac{(n-2)180}{n} = 160\)! Instantly find the exterior angle: \(180^\circ - 160^\circ = 20^\circ\). Then \(n = \frac{360^\circ}{20^\circ} = 18\) sides in 2 seconds!
• Total Diagonals Formula: Total diagonals in an \(n\)-gon \(= \frac{n(n - 3)}{2}\). (A pentagon has \(\frac{5(2)}{2} = 5\) diagonals; an octagon has \(\frac{8(5)}{2} = 20\) diagonals!).

Geometric Proof: Triangulation of an \(n\)-sided Polygon

Why does any \(n\)-sided polygon have an interior angle sum of \((n - 2) \times 180^\circ\)?

1. Choose any single vertex \(V_1\) of an \(n\)-gon.
2. Draw all straight lines (diagonals) from \(V_1\) to all non-adjacent vertices.
3. The polygon is sliced cleanly into exactly \((n - 2)\) non-overlapping triangles!
4. Each triangle's angles add to \(180^\circ\).
5. Summing all triangle angles accounts for every interior angle of the polygon without gaps:
\[\text{Total Sum} = (n - 2) \times 180^\circ \quad \blacksquare\]

Worked Examples: Real Exam Scenarios

Example 1: Parallelogram Angle Relations
• In parallelogram \(ABCD\), two adjacent angles are in the ratio \(3 : 2\). Find all 4 angles.
• Adjacent angles in any parallelogram sum to \(180^\circ\) (supplementary interior angles between parallel lines).
• Let angles be \(3x\) and \(2x\): \(3x + 2x = 180^\circ \implies 5x = 180^\circ \implies x = 36^\circ\).
• Therefore, \(\angle A = 3(36^\circ) = 108^\circ\) and \(\angle B = 2(36^\circ) = 72^\circ\).
• Opposite angles are equal: \(\angle C = 108^\circ\) and \(\angle D = 72^\circ\).
✓ All angles: \(108^\circ, 72^\circ, 108^\circ, 72^\circ\)
Example 2: Rhombus Diagonals & Side Length
• The diagonals of a rhombus are \(16\text{ cm}\) and \(12\text{ cm}\). Find its side length and perimeter.
• Diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles (\(90^\circ\)).
• Half-diagonals: \(d_1/2 = 8\text{ cm}\), \(d_2/2 = 6\text{ cm}\).
• Apply Pythagoras theorem to one quarter-triangle: \(\text{side}^2 = 8^2 + 6^2 = 64 + 36 = 100\).
• Side \(= \sqrt{100} = 10\text{ cm}\). Perimeter \(= 4 \times 10 = 40\text{ cm}\).
✓ Side length \(= 10\text{ cm}\); Perimeter \(= 40\text{ cm}\)

Topic 8: Comparing Quantities & Money

Profit & loss percentages, discount traps, sales tax/GST & compound interest

Core Financial Formulae (Percentages, Discounts & Interest)

  • Profit & Loss \(\text{Profit} = SP - CP\), \(\text{Loss} = CP - SP\). Both \(\text{Profit \%}\) and \(\text{Loss \%}\) are ALWAYS calculated on Cost Price (CP): \(\frac{\text{Profit}}{CP} \times 100\).
  • Discount & MP \(\text{Discount} = \text{Marked Price (MP)} - SP\). Discount \% is ALWAYS calculated on Marked Price: \(\frac{\text{Discount}}{MP} \times 100\).
  • Simple Interest \(SI = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}\),  Total Amount \(A = P + SI\). (Interest is only earned on principal).
  • Compound Interest Compounded Annually: \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^n\).  \(CI = A - P\). Interest earns its own interest!
  • Half-Yearly Compounding When compounded semi-annually, rate is halved (\(\frac{R}{2}\)) and time periods are doubled (\(2n\)): \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{200}\right)^{2n}\).
⚡ Classmate's Shortcut: The Successive Discount Formula & Rule of 72

• The Successive Discount Scam: If a store advertises "20% + 10% OFF", you are NOT getting 30% off! The single equivalent discount is:
\[D_{\text{net}} = d_1 + d_2 - \frac{d_1 \times d_2}{100} = 20 + 10 - \frac{200}{100} = 28\% \text{ discount!}\]
• The Rule of 72: Divide 72 by the annual interest rate \(R\) to see approximately how many years it takes for your investment to double under compound interest! (At \(8\%\), money doubles in \(\approx \frac{72}{8} = 9\) years!).

Why Compound Interest Snowballs (The Mathematical Derivation)

Unlike Simple Interest which stays flat, Compound Interest reinvests every penny earned:

Let principal be \(P\) and interest rate be \(r = \frac{R}{100}\):
• End of Year 1: Amount \(A_1 = P + Pr = P(1 + r)\).
• Year 2 Principal is now \(A_1\)!
• End of Year 2: Amount \(A_2 = A_1 + A_1 r = A_1(1 + r) = [P(1 + r)](1 + r) = P(1 + r)^2\).
• End of Year 3: \(A_3 = P(1 + r)^3\).
• By induction, after \(n\) cycles: \(A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^n \quad \blacksquare\)

Worked Examples: Tested Exam Calculations

Example 1: Discount & Sale Price
• A pair of roller skates was bought at a sale where the discount given was \(20\%\). If the amount paid is \(\$1,600\), find the marked price.
• Let Marked Price be \(M\). Discount \(= 20\%\) of \(M = 0.20M\).
• Selling Price \(= M - 0.20M = 0.80M = 1600\).
• Marked Price \(M = \frac{1600}{0.80} = \$2,000\).
✓ Marked Price \(= \$2,000\)
Example 2: CI vs SI Direct Comparison
• Calculate the difference between Compound Interest and Simple Interest on \(\$12,500\) for \(3\) years at \(12\%\) p.a.
• \(SI = \frac{12500 \times 12 \times 3}{100} = \$4,500\).
• \(CI: A = 12500\left(1 + \frac{12}{100}\right)^3 = 12500 \times (1.12)^3 = 12500 \times 1.404928 = \$17,561.60\).
• \(CI = 17561.60 - 12500 = \$5,061.60\).
• Difference \(= 5061.60 - 4500 = \$561.60\).
✓ Compounding yields \(\$561.60\) extra interest!

Topic 9: Mensuration (2D & 3D Solids)

Trapeziums, general polygons, cubes, cuboids, cylinders & liquid capacity conversion

Essential Mensuration Formulae (Area, Surface & Volume)

  • Area of Trapezium \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h\)  (Half of the sum of parallel sides multiplied by perpendicular height).
  • General Quadrilateral \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} d (h_1 + h_2)\)  where \(d\) is the diagonal and \(h_1, h_2\) are perpendicular offsets from opposite vertices.
  • Cuboid (l, b, h) Lateral Surface Area \(= 2h(l + b)\) • Total Surface Area \(= 2(lb + bh + hl)\) • Volume \(= l \times b \times h\).
  • Cube (Side a) Lateral Surface Area \(= 4a^2\) • Total Surface Area \(= 6a^2\) • Volume \(= a^3\).
  • Cylinder (r, h) Curved Surface Area \(= 2\pi rh\) • Total Surface Area \(= 2\pi r(r + h)\) • Volume \(= \pi r^2 h\).
  • Capacity Units \(1\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ mL}\) • \(1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ Litre}\) • \(1\text{ m}^3 = 1000\text{ Litres} = 1\text{ kL}\).
⚡ Classmate's Shortcut: The 4-Walls Painting Formula & Unrolled Cylinder

• Area of 4 Walls of a Room: \(\text{Area} = 2h(l + b) = \text{Perimeter of floor} \times \text{Height}\)! Save time in exam word problems.
• Unrolling a Cylinder: If you slice a hollow cylinder down its side and unroll it, it turns into a rectangle! The base perimeter becomes the length (\(2\pi r\)) and height is the width (\(h\)). That's why \(\text{CSA} = 2\pi rh\) without memorization!

Why Area of Trapezium is \(\frac{1}{2}(a + b)h\) (Triangle Decomposition)

Take trapezium \(ABCD\) with parallel sides \(AB = a\) and \(CD = b\), and altitude \(h\):

1. Draw diagonal \(AC\) cutting the trapezium into two non-overlapping triangles: \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle ACD\).
2. Both triangles share the exact same perpendicular height \(h\) between the two parallel rails.
3. \(\text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} a h\).
4. \(\text{Area}(\triangle ACD) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} b h\).
5. Summing both areas:
\[\text{Total Area} = \frac{1}{2} a h + \frac{1}{2} b h = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)h \quad \blacksquare\]

Worked Examples: Practical Mensuration

Example 1: Trapezium Field Area
• The parallel sides of a trapezium are \(25\text{ m}\) and \(10\text{ m}\). Its non-parallel sides are both \(14\text{ m}\). The height is \(12\text{ m}\). Find its area.
• Formula: \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} (a + b) h\).
• Substitute: \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} (25 + 10) \times 12 = \frac{1}{2} \times 35 \times 12 = 35 \times 6 = 210\text{ m}^2\).
✓ Field Area \(= 210\text{ m}^2\)
Example 2: Cylindrical Water Tank Capacity
• A cylindrical water tank has diameter \(2.8\text{ m}\) and height \(7\text{ m}\). Find volume and capacity in litres.
• Radius \(r = 2.8 / 2 = 1.4\text{ m}\).
• Volume \(V = \pi r^2 h = \frac{22}{7} \times 1.4 \times 1.4 \times 7 = 22 \times 1.96 = 43.12\text{ m}^3\).
• Since \(1\text{ m}^3 = 1,000\text{ Litres}\), Capacity \(= 43.12 \times 1000 = 43,120\text{ Litres}\).
✓ Capacity \(= 43,120\text{ Litres}\)

Topic 10: Data Handling & Probability

Central angle pie charts, frequency tables, histograms & Monte Carlo probability

Core Principles of Data Representation & Chance

  • Pie Chart Central Angle \(\text{Central Angle} = \left(\frac{\text{Component Frequency}}{\text{Total Sum of Frequencies}}\right) \times 360^\circ\). Sum of all sector angles \(= 360^\circ\).
  • Grouped Data Intervals Class Size \(= \text{Upper Limit} - \text{Lower Limit}\). Class Mark (Midpoint) \(= \frac{\text{Upper Limit} + \text{Lower Limit}}{2}\).
  • Histogram Graphical representation of continuous grouped data where bars touch with no gaps and bar heights represent frequency.
  • Probability Definition \(P(E) = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of equally likely outcomes}}\).
  • Probability Range \(0 \le P(E) \le 1\). Impossible event: \(P = 0\); Sure event: \(P = 1\); Complement: \(P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E)\).
⚡ Classmate's Shortcut: The 3.6° Percentage Multiplier & Sample Space Powers

• Pie Chart Percentage Shortcut: Never divide by 100 and multiply by 360! Since \(\frac{360}{100} = 3.6\), simply multiply any percentage directly by \(3.6^\circ\)!
E.g., \(25\% = 25 \times 3.6 = 90^\circ\); \(10\% = 36^\circ\); \(5\% = 18^\circ\) in seconds!
• Sample Space Counting: Flipping \(n\) coins produces \(2^n\) total outcomes (3 coins \(= 2^3 = 8\)). Rolling \(n\) standard dice produces \(6^n\) total outcomes (2 dice \(= 6^2 = 36\)).

Law of Large Numbers: Theoretical vs Experimental Probability

When flipping a coin, the theoretical chance of Heads is \(P(H) = \frac{1}{2} = 50\%\):

• In 4 flips, you might get 3 Heads (75% - random variance).
• In 100 flips, Heads will hover closely around 48–52 (48%–52%).
• In 10,000 flips, the experimental ratio converges strictly to \(0.5000\)!
\[\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{\text{Observed Heads}}{N} = \frac{1}{2} = P(H) \quad \blacksquare\]

Individual trials are completely unpredictable, but aggregated trials follow rigorous mathematical certainty.

Worked Examples: Exam Problem Scenarios

Example 1: Pie Chart Sector Angles
• In a survey of 360 people, favorite seasons are: Summer: 90, Rainy: 120, Winter: 150. Find the central angles for a pie chart.
• Total \(= 360\). Central Angle \(= \frac{\text{Component}}{360} \times 360^\circ = \text{Component}^\circ\)!
• Summer Sector \(= 90^\circ\). Rainy Sector \(= 120^\circ\). Winter Sector \(= 150^\circ\).
• Sum check: \(90^\circ + 120^\circ + 150^\circ = 360^\circ\) ✓.
✓ Central Angles: \(90^\circ, 120^\circ, 150^\circ\)
Example 2: 52-Card Deck Probability
• One card is drawn from a well-shuffled pack of 52 playing cards. Find the probability of getting: (i) a King of red colour, (ii) a face card, (iii) a spade.
• Total outcomes \(= 52\).
• (i) Red Kings (Hearts, Diamonds) \(= 2 \implies P = \frac{2}{52} = \frac{1}{26}\).
• (ii) Face cards (J, Q, K \(\times 4\)) \(= 12 \implies P = \frac{12}{52} = \frac{3}{13}\).
• (iii) Spades \(= 13 \implies P = \frac{13}{52} = \frac{1}{4}\).
✓ Probabilities: \(\frac{1}{26}\), \(\frac{3}{13}\), and \(\frac{1}{4}\)
